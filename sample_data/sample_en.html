Lectures On Practice

The Gathering Of The Mind

Volume I > Lectures On Practice > The Gathering Of The Mind

The mind of the ordinary person is scattered across a hundred objects at once, and what is divided in that fashion has very little power. Gather it upon a single point and hold it there, and you will find that the same mind which seemed feeble becomes an instrument of great precision. This gathering is not a gift bestowed upon a few; it is a discipline available to anyone willing to practise it daily.

Concentration is therefore the first of the practical virtues, and every other attainment of the inner life rests upon it. The student who cannot hold attention for a single minute should not despair, but should begin with that minute and lengthen it patiently. Patience here is not weakness; it is the recognition that the mind is trained as a muscle is trained, by repetition and not by violence.

Observe that the restlessness of the mind is fed by desire. Where there is no wanting, there is no wandering, and the attention rests naturally upon whatever is placed before it. For this reason the practice of concentration and the practice of non-attachment are not two disciplines but one discipline seen from two sides.

Some ask whether such training makes a person cold or indifferent to the world. The opposite is true. The scattered mind loves feebly because it loves many things at once; the gathered mind loves with its whole strength. Discipline does not extinguish feeling, it concentrates feeling.

Work Without Bondage

Volume I > Lectures On Practice > Work Without Bondage

It is not work that binds a person, but the attachment to the fruit of the work. Two people may perform the same action side by side, and one is enslaved by it while the other remains entirely free. The difference lies not in the deed but in the relation of the doer to the result.

Therefore work with the whole of your energy, and surrender the outcome. This is not indifference to consequences, which would be laziness wearing the mask of philosophy. It is the willingness to labour with complete seriousness while refusing to make one's peace of mind hostage to a result that was never wholly in one's control.

Duty performed in this spirit becomes a means of liberation rather than a chain. The householder at his accounts and the ascetic at his meditation stand on the same ground, if both have given up the anxious clutching after reward. The world is not an obstacle to the inner life; it is the very field upon which the inner life is practised.

Consider how much of human suffering arises not from labour itself but from the fear of failing at it. Remove that fear, and the same labour becomes light. Strength of this kind is quiet; it does not announce itself, and it is rarely mistaken for strength by those who have never possessed it.

The Veil Of Appearance

Volume II > Discourses On The Self > The Veil Of Appearance

What we call the world is not falsehood, but neither is it what it appears to be. It is appearance taken for substance, the rope mistaken for the serpent in a dim light. The error is not in seeing, but in the conclusion drawn from what is seen.

Illusion of this kind cannot be destroyed by argument, because argument itself operates inside it. It is dissolved by direct knowledge, as the fear of the serpent is dissolved the moment a lamp is brought near. Until then, every proof merely rearranges the darkness.

The self that observes all this is never touched by what it observes. Whatever changes is not the self, for that which knows change must itself stand outside the changing. Realization is nothing more exotic than ceasing to confuse the two.

Freedom, in this account, is not something to be acquired at the end of a long journey. It is what remains when a misunderstanding is removed. The seeker is already what is sought, which is why the search is so often difficult and its conclusion so simple.

Letters On Devotion

Volume II > Discourses On The Self > Letters On Devotion

You ask whether devotion and knowledge are rivals. They are not. Devotion without understanding becomes sentiment, and understanding without devotion becomes dryness; each corrects the characteristic disease of the other.

Love directed toward the infinite does not diminish love directed toward a human being. On the contrary, it steadies it, because it removes from human love the impossible demand that it be everything. Much of what passes for affection is only the fear of loneliness dressed in better clothes.

Do not be discouraged by the coldness you describe in your practice. Dry periods are not evidence of failure; they are the ordinary weather of any long discipline. Continue the practice without measuring it, and the measuring itself will eventually fall away.

Above all, be strong. Weakness is the one thing I would have you renounce first, before you renounce anything else. A timid person cannot be truthful, cannot be generous, and cannot in the end be free, because every virtue requires the courage to sustain it.

A Conversation On Renunciation

Volume II > Discourses On The Self > A Conversation On Renunciation

A visitor asked what must be given up. The teacher replied that nothing external needs to be abandoned by one who has abandoned the desire for it, and no external abandonment helps one who has not.

Renunciation, properly understood, is not the loss of anything valuable. It is the refusal to pay for small things with one's freedom. The person who cannot be bribed by comfort is not poorer than the rest; he is simply not for sale.

The visitor objected that such teaching suits monks and not householders. The teacher answered that the discipline is identical and only its outward form differs, as the same fire cooks in one house and warms in another.

Truth, he added, is not the property of any order of life. It waits equally for anyone who will approach it without flattery and without fear, and it withdraws equally from anyone who will not.
